Overview

Turning a clinical gold standard into a playful mobile game

Lila is a voice-enabled mobile game based on memorization. The project started with a deceptively simple brief: take Cogstate’s “International Shopping List Test”—a gold-standard cognitive assessment used in clinical trials—and transform it into a consumer-friendly mobile experience.

On paper, the ask was straightforward. In reality, the challenge was to preserve clinical rigor and data quality while designing something warm, inviting, and fun enough that everyday users would choose to play it on their own.

Problem & research

Balancing scientific rigor with emotional safety

The original assessment was built for clinicians, not consumers. Language like “test,” “trial,” and “performance” created anxiety, and the stakes of cognitive decline can feel intimidating. We needed to:

  • Lower the emotional barrier to entry for a cognitive assessment experience.
  • Introduce voice interaction in a way that felt natural, not surveillance-heavy.
  • Guide users through environment and device checks without breaking immersion.

After an initial design sprint with Cogstate’s in-house scientists, I interviewed five participants to ground the concepts in real behavior. Research surfaced recurring themes: apprehension about “being tested,” confusion about how voice input would be used, and a desire for simple, reassuring feedback after each attempt.

Flows & structure

Designing the journey from device check to gameplay

I created low-fidelity wireframes to represent primary scenarios: onboarding, device and environment checks, the in-game memorization flow, and post-session feedback. These screens evolved into a clickable prototype that stakeholders could react to early.

Device & environment readiness

Because voice capture quality directly affects assessment value, the device check flow is central to Lila. Working closely with Cogstate’s science team, I mapped a series of system and environment checks that:

  • Confirm microphone access and basic audio quality.
  • Help users move to a quieter space without shaming them for “doing it wrong.”
  • Clearly signal when they are ready to play and what to expect next.

Iterations

Refining language, feedback, and visual tone

Once stakeholders could interact with the prototype, feedback surfaced specific opportunities to refine both UX and UI:

  • Reframing “test” language to feel more like a game session than an exam.
  • Smoothing friction points in device checks so they felt guided instead of punitive.
  • Strengthening positive reinforcement and progress indicators after each round.

I iterated on microcopy, navigation, and affordances while gradually layering in branding and visual design. The goal was a calm, supportive tone that still felt modern and playful.

High-fidelity prototype

With the interaction model validated, I evolved the prototype into a fully branded experience. The visual system supports quick comprehension, reduces anxiety, and keeps the experience feeling like a game rather than a clinical assessment.
