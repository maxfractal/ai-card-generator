01 · Overview

Setting the stage

The modern Olympic Games are not a single product; they’re a constantly shifting constellation of live events, replays, highlights, athlete stories, and medal updates distributed across dozens of platforms. My work at NBCUniversal focused on turning that constellation into a coherent content experience: one shared language, one metadata backbone, and a set of UX writing patterns that could travel anywhere the Games appeared.

I partnered with metadata engineering, Olympics editorial, marketing, Peacock UX, and external distribution partners to define how we name, group, and describe Olympic content so that fans know what they’re getting before they ever hit “Watch.”

02 · Challenge

The problem we needed to solve

Before we aligned on a shared content model, every platform told a slightly different version of the Olympic story. Event names were truncated or inconsistent, “live” and “replay” were labelled in conflicting ways, medal coverage surfaced differently, and terminology for formats and rounds varied across surfaces.

For fans, this meant friction: “Is this live?”, “Is this the full event or just a clip?”, “Where do I see medal standings?”. For partners and internal teams, it meant additional support burden, rushed last-minute fixes, and duplicate work.

Design question How might we create a content and language system that makes Olympic coverage predictable and understandable, no matter where a fan presses play?

03 · My role

What I owned

Content architecture & metadata model

I mapped the full Olympic content lifecycle from scheduling to playout to partner ingestion, then defined a hierarchy for how events, replays, highlights, medal hubs, sport hubs, and athlete hubs relate to one another.

  • Established naming conventions for events, rounds, and sessions.
  • Defined metadata fields required for consistent ingestion and display.
  • Worked with engineering to ensure the model was technically feasible and scalable.

UX writing & language system

I created a shared vocabulary for key fan questions: What is this? Is it live? Is this the whole thing? What happens if I click?

  • Standardized action labels (e.g. Watch Live, Full Replay, Highlights, Medal Recap).
  • Developed patterns for schedule, blackout, and rights messaging in plain language.
  • Wrote guidance for how to describe complex sports formats and multi-session events.

Partner-facing guidelines & playbook

To make the system usable beyond NBCU’s walls, I translated it into a practical “Platform Experience Playbook” for partners and internal teams.

  • Content and copy specs for partner engineering teams.
  • Usage examples showing best- and worst-case implementations.
  • Edge-case handling: what copy appears when schedules change, events overlap, or streams are temporarily unavailable.

04 · Approach

How we designed the system

1. Mapping the ecosystem

I began by interviewing stakeholders across editorial, product, data, engineering, and partner operations. Together we mapped how an event moved: from initial schedule, to live feed, to replay, to highlight reels, to packaged partner feeds.

2. Finding the seams and failure points

We audited how different partners labeled and surfaced Olympic content. We found gaps such as:

  • Inconsistent “Live” and “Replay” indicators.
  • Medal coverage hidden behind generic labels like “News” or “Clips”.
  • Long event titles truncated without context.
  • Confusing or missing copy in cases of blackouts and schedule changes.

3. Designing reusable content patterns

From there, I designed content patterns that could withstand different platform layouts and constraints:

  • Short, descriptive titles with predictable structures (Sport · Round · Country vs Country).
  • Clear sublabels and badges to distinguish Live, Replay, and Highlight.
  • Microcopy templates for medal hubs and athlete pages that balanced storytelling with scannability.

4. Testing and refinement

We reviewed patterns with partner UX teams and internal stakeholders, refining the language and rules until they were both technically implementable and editorially faithful.

05 · Outcomes

What changed for fans & partners

For fans

  • Easier to tell at a glance whether something was live, a full replay, or a short highlight.
  • More predictable navigation into sport hubs, athlete hubs, and medal coverage.
  • Clearer explanations of event formats, so fewer “What am I watching?” moments.

For partners & internal teams

  • Reduced ambiguity in how Olympic content should be labeled and grouped on their platforms.
  • Faster onboarding for partner engineering teams using the shared playbook and examples.
  • A reusable framework for future Games and other tentpole events.
System impact The work shifted NBCUniversal from “fixing language one surface at a time” to operating with a shared content model that can scale across events, partners, and years.
06 · Reflection

What this taught me about content design at scale

This project reinforced that metadata and language are not backstage concerns; they’re the backbone of how fans experience live events. The most visible UI moments depend on invisible foundations: naming systems, content hierarchies, and clear rules about how we talk about the product.

As a content designer working in a product leadership role, I learned how to act as translator and architect: turning editorial ambition into structures that engineering can build, partners can implement, and fans can instantly understand.
